# Fried Liver Attack

The Fried Liver Attack, also called the Fegatello Attack, is 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Nf6 4.Ng5 d5 5.exd5 Nxd5 6.Nxf7. It is a variation of the Two Knights Defence, which arises when Black answers the [[Italian Game]] with 3...Nf6, and it acts on the square the Italian bishop aims at: 4.Ng5 brings the knight against f7 beside the bishop on c4, so f7 is attacked twice. Both 4.Ng5 and 6.Nxf7 move a knight that has already moved, the kind of move the [[Opening principles]] warn against.

6.Nxf7 is not check, but Black effectively has to take, 6...Kxf7, because the knight forks the queen on d8 and the rook on h8. Play usually continues 7.Qf3+ Ke6: the queen checks and adds a second attacker to the knight on d5, which the bishop on c4 pins against the king, so the king has to come forward to hold it. White then goes after the exposed king, typically bringing in Nc3.

White gets a strong attack, but it has not been proven decisive. 5...Nxd5 is risky in practice rather than refuted, and it is most dangerous for Black at short time controls, where defending is harder than attacking. Black's sound answer is to avoid the line: 5...Na5 is the standard reply, generally considered best, and engines rate the result equal.

At move six White has an alternative, 6.d4, the Lolli Attack, which is often regarded as the stronger try. Bobby Fischer rated it highly in *My 60 Memorable Games* (game 45, Fischer–Bisguier, 1963). It is not settled, though: a 2008 article argues that neither attack troubles a Black player who is prepared for it.

The Fried Liver is common at club level and almost never seen among grandmasters, because it relies on Black going wrong. That claim rests only on undated popular sources, not on an authoritative one.
