# Lucena position recaps

## Step 1 — Recognising the Lucena position

The Lucena position is a rook-and-pawn-against-rook endgame in which the side with the pawn has a known way to win. In the standard position White has king d8, pawn d7 and rook f1, and Black has king g7 and rook c2. A position is a Lucena when five things hold: the pawn is on its seventh rank and not on an edge file; the attacking king stands on the square in front of it; the defending king is cut off from the pawn by the file the attacking rook holds; the defending rook stops the king leaving on the other side; and the attacking rook is free to move. What counts is how the pieces stand relative to each other, not the exact squares. White's problem is that its own king blocks the pawn, and a king that steps out on the open side can be checked from behind by the defending rook.

## Step 2 — Why the cut-off matters

White's pawn can't move while its own king stands on the promotion square, so the king has to step out, and from d8 it can only go sideways: to c7 or c8, which Black's rook on the c-file shuts, or to e7 or e8. Those two squares are what the cut-off protects. A rook's file stops the defending king crossing it, but not covering squares on the far side from right beside it, since two kings can never stand on neighbouring squares. In the standard position the wall is the f-file, so Black's king can never touch e7 or e8. Move Black's king a file nearer, to f7, with the wall on the e-file, and every checklist feature still holds, yet White's king has no legal move; White would first have to drive the king further off, which the tutorial doesn't follow. The cut-off that matters keeps the defending king away from the squares beside the pawn.

## Step 3 — Building the bridge

From the standard position White wins with 1.Rf4 Rc1 2.Ke7 Re1+ 3.Kd6 Rd1+ 4.Ke6 Re1+ 5.Kd5 Rd1+ 6.Rd4, in four stages: rook up, king out, king down, rook across. The rook goes to the fourth rank because it will later block a check on a square its king protects, and the fourth is the highest rank Black's king, on g7, can't attack in one move. The king steps out onto e7, the square the cut-off keeps open, and Black's rook checks it from far behind, where the rook is safe. The king zigzags down away from each check, staying beside its pawn while Black's rook is on the pawn's file, and reaches d5. Then the rook slides to d4 and blocks, leaving Black's rook no open line to check along. If Black takes the bridge, 6...Rxd4+ 7.Kxd4, its king is still two moves from the queening square and the pawn promotes. The picture to keep is the finished bridge: the rook laid across the fourth rank with White's king standing on it.

## Step 4 — Where it applies and where it doesn't

The bridge works the same way on the mirrored board and for a pawn on any file but the edge: the rook climbs its own file to the fourth rank, the king steps out on the side away from the defending rook and zigzags down, and the rook blocks on the pawn's own file with the king standing on it. Mirrored, left and right swap but the ranks don't; with the pawn as Black, count ranks from your own side. A pawn on the a- or h-file breaks the method, because its king has only one side to step out on and the defending rook shuts it; offering a rook trade there can even leave White stalemated, since the pawn is blocked by its own king and the cornered king's only other squares are on the file Black's rook then holds. When the defending king isn't cut off far enough, it isn't a Lucena, and the defender has drawing methods of its own: the Philidor position, when the king gets in front of the pawn, and checks from the side. Aim for the Lucena when the pawn is off the edge and the defending king can be cut off; as the defender, keep your king from being cut off.
